Case 01

Mixed Methods Feedback at Scale Open-Ended Coding

What 3,467 People Mean When They Say “Biased”

Turning a one-word complaint into something a team can act on

Published in Journalism (2026) · 1st Place Paper, AEJMC 2024 · with G. Masullo & M. Duchovnay, Center for Media Engagement

The Question

Millions of people say the news is “biased.” Newsrooms kept answering the textbook definition — imbalanced coverage — instead of what distrustful audiences actually meant. It is the kind of one-word complaint every feedback system collects and almost none can act on. We set out to map the folk theories behind the word.

My Role

Researcher on a three-person team — built the data-quality pipeline, coded open-ended responses, analyzed interview transcripts, and synthesized the folk theories that explain the survey’s trust rankings.

Approach

  1. Screened 8,514 raw survey responses down to 3,467 valid cases — bot detection, duplicate screening, quality filtering.
  2. Coded open-ended trust responses to reliability (Krippendorff’s α = .92).
  3. Analyzed 91 interview transcripts, grounded-theory-informed, across a 27-newsroom field partnership.
  4. Synthesized the folk theories that explain why the survey’s trust rankings looked the way they did.
8,514 → 3,467
responses cleaned to valid cases · 91 interviews

What I Found

  • “Biased” mostly meant “I don’t see myself” — content read as biased when a reader’s perspective was absent or portrayed negatively, even when it was accurate.
  • Single “bogeyman” words flipped an entire piece from information to manipulation. Distrust operated on lexical cues, not arguments.
  • Accuracy alone did not produce perceived legitimacy.

Recommendations

  • Code the theory behind the complaint, don’t just count it — “biased,” “unfair,” and “wrong” each encode a definable logic that makes the fix testable.
  • Test the exact wording of labels, notices, and responses with low-trust segments — terms that read neutral internally can read as signals of intent.
  • Treat representation in examples and content as a trust lever, not only an inclusion value.

So whatFor a customer-feedback or VoC team: a one-word complaint is a mental model, not a sentiment score. Map the model and the response becomes something you can design and measure.

ReflectionThe first finding in any feedback dataset is how much of it isn’t real — bot and duplicate screening changed the trust rankings before a single theme was coded.

Case 02

Qualitative Generative

Safe Enough to Speak

What makes marginalized users participate in online communities

The Question

Mainstream comment spaces are often hostile, and marginalized users leave first. I studied the inverse: what makes a community feel safe enough to participate — and what design conditions create that feeling?

My Role

Sole researcher — scoping, recruitment, discussion guide, interviews, analysis, synthesis.

Approach

  1. Screened the top LGBTQIA+ communities (Muck Rack) down to nine active forums.
  2. Recruited via a 6.9k-member group: 204 applicants → 22 users selected.
  3. Designed a 36-question discussion guide, refined with three experts.
  4. Ran ~45-min sessions; analyzed via a seven-step thematic coding process.
22
users interviewed, from 204 screened applicants

What I Found

  • Perceived similarity (18/22) was the strongest driver of participation.
  • Reciprocity (14/22) sustained active engagement over time.
  • Users who felt similarity rated the space as safe and civil (20/22).

Recommendations

  • Treat safety as a perception problem, not only moderation — make community composition and shared-identity signals visible.
  • Surface similarity signals (onboarding, profiles, matching) to convert lurkers into contributors.
  • Weigh the homogeneity tradeoff (comfort vs. lost cross-cutting exposure) as a monitored platform-health risk.

So whatFor a community or support product, participation is a perceived-safety metric as much as an interest metric — design the safety signals, not only the moderation.

ReflectionRandom selection converged on a single racial group — I now build quota-based screening in up front.

Case 03

Quantitative Survey Modeling

Same Distrust, Different Roots

Why one-size-fits-all fixes fail

The Question

When users lose confidence in a system, the instinct is one universal fix. But what if different segments distrust for entirely different reasons? I tested whether one behavior had one cause — or many.

My Role

Lead researcher and first author — survey design, measurement, modeling, interpretation.

Approach

  1. Fielded a survey of 1,294 U.S. adults, quota-matched to the population.
  2. Measured each construct with validated multi-item scales; checked reliability.
  3. Modeled with SEM + bootstrapped mediation (5,000 resamples).
  4. Re-ran the model by segment to test whether mechanisms held.
1,294
participants · 5,000 bootstrap resamples

What I Found

  • On the surface distrust looked uniform — one shared driver lowered confidence everywhere.
  • But mechanisms diverged by segment: one group's distrust ran through how they evaluate information; another's did not.
  • Self-reported "skill" predicted overconfidence, not accuracy.

Recommendations

  • Segment before you intervene — the same behavior with different roots needs different solutions.
  • Don't take self-reported competence at face value.
  • Aggregate metrics can hide opposing segment effects — always disaggregate.

So whatFor any product with a single "trust" KPI: one aggregate number can hide two segments that need opposite fixes.

ReflectionCross-sectional, post-election data limited causal claims; next: longitudinal + behavioral (not self-report) measures.

Case 04

Mixed Methods Survey + Interviews

Trust Is Not the Same as Reliance

Navigating information inside communities you're leaving

The Question

Teams assume that if users trust a source they rely on it, and if they distrust it they leave. I studied people living with that tension to map the real relationship between trust, reliance, and voice.

My Role

Sole researcher — entire study, both phases, end to end (doctoral capstone).

Approach

  1. Fielded a national survey of 305 women across Christian traditions.
  2. Measured trust in sources, digital + offline engagement, attitudes.
  3. Ran 20 in-depth interviews (purposive, maximum-variation sampling).
  4. Survey mapped what patterns existed; interviews explained why.
305 + 20
surveyed, then interviewed in depth

What I Found

  • A trust–reliance gap: people relied on sources they trusted less, and trusted sources they used less.
  • Compensatory engagement: those silent in their core community were the most active elsewhere.
  • Digital spaces became transition infrastructure — where people rehearsed and enacted leaving.

Recommendations

  • Measure reliance and trust separately — a single "trust" score misleads product decisions.
  • Track where suppressed behavior goes, not just where it stops.
  • Design for users in transition, not only steady states — that support earns deep loyalty.

So whatFor a feedback or engagement tool: users who go quiet are not necessarily gone — measure where behavior moves, not only where it stops.

ReflectionStudying a sensitive population as an outsider sharpened how I scope recruitment and build trust with vulnerable users.

Case 05

Mixed Methods Growth Experiments Community Listening

From Comments to Decisions

What happens when the researcher is also the one who ships

Side project · Instagram @olihasopinions · launched Sep 28, 2026

The Question

My other cases end where most academic research ends: with recommendations. I wanted to own the whole loop — listen to an audience, form an insight, ship a change, and see whether the numbers moved. So I started an Instagram account for my rescue cat, Oli, and treated every post as a small study.

My Role

Sole researcher, strategist and creator — audience analysis, content decisions, production (a labeled AI video series plus real footage), and measurement.

Approach

  1. Tracked views, interactions, shares and profile visits per post in Instagram Insights.
  2. Read comments on top posts across 6+ languages and grouped them into recurring themes.
  3. Changed one element at a time between episodes (ending, format, text size) and compared results against the account’s baseline.
  4. Fed recurring comment themes directly into the next piece of content.
0 → 25.9K
followers in 7 weeks · 7.6M views · 97% from non-followers

What I Found → What I Changed

  • Endings drove sharing. Early endings that introduced a new situation felt flat. I switched to endings that paid off the setup with a cost-of-living joke (a $60 loaf, cash only). The next episode reached 5.8M views and 279K shares; the pinned reply “no refunds” alone drew 5.9K likes.
  • One format earned a higher baseline. I made the job-series format the account’s identity on Oct 10. Most earlier posts drew 10–40K views; later episodes have consistently drawn 90K–270K.
  • People treated a fictional shop as a real one. Comments on the first episode read like customer complaints (“stale bread,” “where’s the cheese?”). I answered them in character and built the follow-up episode on them.
  • The audience was older than I assumed. Most viewers were 40+, many 60+, so I enlarged all on-screen text and remade a carousel that was unreadable on a phone.
  • Warm posts invited stories, not just likes. Followers replied with their own cats’ rescue stories, so I launched a weekly series that features followers’ rescued cats.

Recommendations

  • Treat repeated complaints as a backlog, not noise. They tell you what people expect the product to do.
  • Change one thing at a time and compare against the baseline, not against the best day.
  • Check who your audience actually is before designing for an imagined one.

OutcomeBrand partnership offers within seven weeks of launch.

So whatFor any consumer product: comments, support tickets and social replies are a free listening loop. Code them, act on them in small steps, and report the baseline lift separately from the viral spike.

ReflectionOne post accounts for about 75% of all views, so total views overstate the effect of any single change. The honest metric is baseline lift. There was no control group either; next I’d use Instagram’s trial reels to A/B-test one change against non-followers before posting it.
